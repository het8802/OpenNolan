See it in action

A video hook is the first roughly one second of a short-form video — the moment that stops a thumb mid-scroll. It works one of two ways: it shows the payoff up front (the result, the surprise, the punchline) before any setup, or it uses a pattern interrupt (an unexpected image, motion, or claim) that the brain can't ignore. Everything after the hook only matters if the hook earns the watch.

Here's the part that stings for builders. You can have the best product on the feed and still get skipped, because the videos that travel don't open the way ours instinctively do. We start with context — "hey, so I've been working on this thing" — and by the time we reach the good part, the viewer is already three videos away. The first second was the whole game, and we spent it warming up.

We built OpenNolan because we're builders too, and we'd rather ship product than sit in a timeline relearning this every week. This page is the plain-English version of what a hook is and how to build one — and at the end, how the agent opens your reel on the hook automatically so you don't have to think about it.

Why the first second matters more than the rest

The first second isn't the intro to the video. It is the video, as far as the scroll is concerned.

Short-form platforms decide whether to push your video based on early signals — and the earliest, strongest signal is whether people keep watching past the opening. A scroller gives you a fraction of a second to answer one question: is this worth my attention? If the answer isn't obvious before the first sentence finishes, they're gone, and the algorithm reads that as "don't show this to more people."

This is why a weak hook quietly caps everything downstream. Your edit can be tight, your captions clean, your product genuinely great — none of it gets seen if the first second doesn't land. Fixing the hook is the single highest-leverage change you can make to a video that isn't getting reach.

The five hook types that actually stop the scroll

Most strong hooks fall into one of a handful of shapes. You don't need all of them — you need one, executed in the first second. Reach for whichever fits the clip you already have.

  • Payoff-first: open on the result or the surprise, then explain how you got there. "This took 4 hours to build" over the finished thing — not a slow lead-up to it.
  • Pattern interrupt: an unexpected visual, motion, or cut that breaks the feed's rhythm and makes the eye stop before the brain decides to.
  • Bold claim: a confident, specific statement that's slightly hard to believe — so people stay to see if you back it up.
  • Question: name the exact problem your viewer has out loud, so they feel seen and need the answer.
  • Visual hook: a striking on-screen image — a chart spiking, a before/after, a screenshot with the number highlighted — that carries the message with zero words.

Hook examples for founders (and the mistakes to skip)

Concrete beats abstract. If you're shipping a product, your hook usually lives inside something you already have on screen: a metric, a demo moment, a reaction. "We hit 10k users with no marketing team" (bold claim), "watch what happens when you paste a messy spreadsheet here" (payoff-first on the demo), or simply opening on the dashboard with the key number already highlighted (visual). Each one earns the next two seconds.

The mistakes are predictable because almost everyone makes them. Don't open with a logo or a title card — that's a stop sign, not a hook. Don't say "hi guys, in this video" — you've spent the only second that mattered on a greeting. Don't bury the interesting part at second eight; move it to second zero. And don't whisper a bold claim quietly — a strong hook is delivered like you believe it.

Frequently asked questions

What is a video hook in simple terms?

A video hook is the first one second or so of a short-form video — the bit that decides whether someone stops scrolling. A strong hook leads with the payoff (the result or surprise) or uses a pattern interrupt to grab attention before any setup. If the hook doesn't land, the rest of the video barely gets watched, no matter how good it is.

How long should a video hook be?

Under a second to grab attention, and ideally the first three seconds to hold it. People decide whether to keep watching almost instantly, so the most interesting thing — your claim, your result, your visual — should be on screen immediately. Don't spend that first second on a greeting, a logo, or a slow lead-up to the good part.

What makes a bad hook?

The usual culprits: opening on a logo or title card, starting with "hi guys, in this video," burying the interesting moment several seconds in, or delivering a bold claim too quietly to register. Each one wastes the only second that decides your reach. A good hook moves the payoff to the very front and delivers it like you mean it.
