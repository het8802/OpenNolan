# Hooks, Formats, and Angles

Use this reference when choosing an opening, drafting title text, selecting a format, or diagnosing why a Reel is not earning an initial watch.

## Build a three-part hook stack

Every opening has three surfaces. Make them reinforce one claim.

| Surface | Job | Test |
| --- | --- | --- |
| On-screen text | Communicate the subject and tension at a glance. | Can a muted viewer describe the promise? |
| Spoken line | Add specificity, voice, and a reason to stay. | Does it sharpen rather than repeat the text? |
| First visual | Make the claim concrete or create a relevant interruption. | Would this frame still fit the same idea with no audio? |

Prioritize clarity over cleverness. A usable text hook usually identifies the subject, action, desired state, and contrast. Add a specific time frame, constraint, proof cue, or exclusivity cue only if it is true.

## Six opening families

Use one family as the dominant opening mechanism. Do not stack several unrelated ideas into the first sentence.

| Family | Core move | Appropriate use | Generic pattern |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Future change | Signal a credible coming shift in the viewer’s world. | New tools, rules, behavior shifts, forecasts. | “This changes how [audience] approaches [job].” |
| Contrarian | Challenge an accepted tactic with a defensible alternative. | The common advice is incomplete or wrong in context. | “Stop [habit]; for [audience], it causes [cost].” |
| Insider | Reveal non-obvious process knowledge or a hidden constraint. | You have genuine access, experience, or a well-supported observation. | “What people miss about [topic] is [insight].” |
| Proven winner | Lead with relevant evidence, then explain the mechanism. | You can show a result, demo, comparison, or case. | “We changed [input] and saw [specific outcome].” |
| Eye magnet | Begin with an unusual, legible, or visually satisfying object/action. | The visual itself advances the claim. | Show the thing, then name why it matters. |
| Connector | Place two normally separate concepts together to create a question. | The connection produces a real insight, not clickbait. | “What [unexpected thing] teaches [audience] about [job].” |

## Angle library

An angle is the lens that makes a familiar topic worth revisiting. Pair one with a topic and a proof source.

- **New reveal:** identify a real change, overlooked detail, or new fact.
- **Contrast framing:** position the reveal against the old default, a false belief, or the cost of inaction.
- **Urgency:** explain why timing matters only when it actually does.
- **Bullseye proof:** give the precise evidence that makes the claim credible to this audience.
- **Transformation:** show a move from a starting state to a desired state.
- **Diagnostic:** name the symptom, identify the hidden cause, then give the correction.
- **Villain/hero:** frame the new approach against an outdated method, false belief, or shared frustration.
- **Reference connection:** derive an insight by comparing two apparently unrelated domains.

Avoid a claim that is merely louder. The angle must alter how the viewer understands or acts on the topic.

## Visual-hook modes

Use one of these modes to make the first frame do real editorial work:

1. **Graphic or text overlay:** show a concise claim, diagram, scorecard, or comparison.
2. **Pattern interrupt:** change scale, setting, camera grammar, or expected sequence in a way that relates to the point.
3. **Subject motion:** begin with purposeful hand, body, object, or camera movement.
4. **Visual effect:** use an effect to clarify contrast, transformation, timing, or a thought experiment.
5. **Unique visual selection:** choose a scene, object, artifact, screen, or demonstration the viewer has a reason to inspect.

Do not use visual novelty as a substitute for a claim. The first frame should set up the payoff rather than conceal it.

## Format selection

Choose a format that matches the proof and production capacity.

| Format | Best evidence | Typical tension | Common failure |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Breakdown | Framework, observation, or artifact | “What actually drives this?” | Listing steps without a point of view. |
| Ranking | Comparative criteria and examples | “Which option matters most?” | Arbitrary order or unsupported judgment. |
| Case study | Before/after, process, or decision trail | “How did this outcome happen?” | Hiding the constraints or proof. |
| Scenario | A recognizable moment or counterfactual | “What would I do in this situation?” | Acting without an applicable lesson. |
| Demonstration | Live process, screen, object, or skill | “Can this actually work?” | Slow setup before the proof appears. |
| Myth reversal | Clear old belief and better alternative | “Have I been doing this wrong?” | Manufacturing a strawman. |
| Story | Character, stakes, change, and lesson | “What happens next?” | No central tension or payoff. |

## Hook revision checklist

- Name a recognizable subject or audience.
- State the action, change, cost, or outcome.
- Add contrast only when the difference is meaningful.
- Make the first visual prove, preview, or intensify the line.
- Remove generic superlatives and promises that cannot be substantiated.
- Write at least three alternatives from different opening families before choosing one.
